## Page job

Help keepers improve environmental complexity and behavioral opportunity while screening proposed “toys” for fin damage, entrapment, forced contact, chronic reflection and other welfare risks.

The article must distinguish **environmental enrichment** from gimmicks. It should never imply that every novel object is beneficial or that a fish must interact with a toy to be healthy.

## Unique promise

Provide an evidence-led method for choosing, introducing, observing and removing enrichment rather than a shopping list of products.

## Evidence base

### Existing registered sources

- **SRC-041** — Betta tank-size and furnishing study. Furnished environments were associated with more swimming/foraging and less stereotypic or abnormal behavior than barren conditions; fish frequently used furnishings for resting. The authors also emphasized individual variation.
- **SRC-042** — Space and environmental-enrichment study. Physical enrichment increased swimming and behavioral expression compared with bare conditions; larger/enriched housing did not make male cohabitation safe.
- **SRC-035 / SRC-037** — Mirror-response studies. Reflections are aggression stimuli with substantial individual variation; they are not evidence for a mandatory “exercise” routine.
- **SRC-048 / SRC-049** — Plant references useful for safe, realistic live-plant context without claiming injury-proof layouts.

### Newly registered source

- **SRC-074** — 2026 Betta tactile-stimulation experiment. Fish interacted with the device, but the treatment increased stress-related and aggression-related responses rather than producing the expected welfare benefit. This is a key boundary: **interaction does not prove benefit** and enrichment effects must be evaluated by species and individual response.

## Key evidence conclusions

1. A barren environment can restrict behavioral opportunities; physical complexity such as plants, substrate/refuges and usable resting areas can support broader behavioral expression.
2. Enrichment is not synonymous with “toy.”
3. More objects are not automatically better; keep swimming routes, surface access and maintenance access open.
4. A fish interacting with an object does not prove the object is beneficial.
5. Betta responses vary among individuals.
6. Mirrors should be treated as aggression stimuli, not default daily enrichment.
7. Forced tactile contact or “self-brushing” devices should not be recommended as a welfare intervention based on current Betta evidence.
8. Novel items should be introduced conservatively, observed, and removed when they cause persistent flaring, frantic avoidance, trapping, fin damage or restricted access to food/surface/resting areas.

## Proposed article structure

### H1 candidate
**Betta Fish Enrichment: Safe Ideas, Toys to Avoid & How to Judge Your Fish’s Response**

### Intro
Define enrichment and state that the goal is appropriate choice and behavioral opportunity—not constant stimulation.

### Section 1 — What counts as enrichment for a betta?
Explain physical/environmental complexity, resting places and exploration opportunities.

### Section 2 — Evidence-supported starting points
Prioritize:
- live/safe plants;
- refuges/hides with safe openings;
- open swimming routes;
- resting locations at different heights;
- environmental variety that does not obstruct the surface.

Keep this principles-based, not product-ranked.

### Section 3 — Are betta “toys” safe?
Introduce a screening checklist:
- no sharp/rough edges;
- no small trapping openings;
- no paint/material shedding;
- no forced body contact;
- no persistent reflective surface;
- no blockage of filter/heater/surface access;
- item can be removed easily.

### Section 4 — Mirrors are not default enrichment
Hand off to `/behavior/flaring-triggers/`. Do not repeat the mirror article.

### Section 5 — Why interaction does not prove benefit
Use SRC-074 as the central evidence boundary. A fish may repeatedly contact an object and still show increased stress-related physiology/behavior.

### Section 6 — How to introduce and evaluate a new object
Suggested observation framework:
1. add one meaningful change at a time;
2. observe exploration, normal swimming, feeding and resting;
3. watch for persistent flaring, avoidance, frantic movement, trapped fins/body or loss of normal access;
4. remove the item if negative responses persist;
5. do not keep reintroducing a clearly aversive stimulus as “training.”

### Section 7 — What not to use as enrichment
Bounded categories:
- continuous mirrors/reflections;
- another male/female betta as “company”;
- tight tunnels/holes;
- rough or sharp decor;
- objects requiring forced rubbing/contact;
- food activities that encourage excess feeding;
- anything that compromises water quality or maintenance.

## Claim boundaries

Do not state:
- “Bettas need toys to be happy.”
- “This product cures boredom.”
- “A mirror is required exercise.”
- “If the fish touches or uses an object, it likes it.”
- “Tactile stimulation reduces Betta stress.”
- “Add another fish for social enrichment.”
- A universal rotation schedule.
- A numeric enrichment quota.
- Product rankings without an approved testing methodology.

Avoid anthropomorphic diagnosis such as “depressed,” “lonely” or “bored” based on one behavior. Describe observable behavior instead.
